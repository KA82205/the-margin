How the forecast is made

What goes into the number, why each part counts as much as it does, and how the forecast will sharpen as the election gets closer.

What goes into the number

Three kinds of evidence. Polls and fundamentals set the national number. State models and each state's voting history place the states around it. Betting markets are shown beside the forecast for comparison and do not move it.

Why these weights?

Every weight was written down before the back-test was scored, with a reason, and then each one was changed to see whether it mattered. Mostly it did not: across every alternative tried, the typical state miss on the last day stayed between and points.

Three sites, three questions

The Margin reads two sister sites, and neither of them reads The Margin. Each answers a different question, so the three figures below are not the same kind of number: a count of conditions is not a chance of winning, and a share of the vote is not one either.

Sources and limits

The Margin is a personal, non-commercial project. It checks its sources many times a day and reruns the forecast when a poll or one of its sources has changed. Polls of past elections come from FiveThirtyEight's archive and the model data The Economist published; polling averages for 1972 to 2016 come from FiveThirtyEight's public data; expert ratings are Cook Political Report's and Sabato's Crystal Ball's; results are official counts. The fundamentals use the Fifty-Two Keys count, and the national head-to-head polls, state starting points and presidential approval come from the Tipping Point Nowcast, which reads its polls from VoteHub and from PollResults.org. Polls of single states are read from PollResults.org, which serves The New York Times's poll data. Both sets of poll data are used under the CC BY 4.0 license; The Margin is not affiliated with or endorsed by VoteHub, PollResults.org or The New York Times. The code and data are in the repository.

Limits worth knowing: pollsters are not rated, so each counts the same apart from sample size and recency. Betting odds are shown for comparison and are not in the forecast or the back-tests: election-eve odds for 2008 to 2024 have not been collected from sources that can be checked, so their worth has not been tested. The Fifty-Two Keys count for a past year uses that year's final data. The back-tests include Tipping Point's state-level models, but with one forecast for each year standing in for every day of it, and for 2020 the approval model's entry is the forecast its authors published then, made with an earlier version of the model.
