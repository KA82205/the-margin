How the forecasts are made

The Margin runs two models, one for the presidency and one for the Senate. Each is explained here in full: what goes into it, how much each part counts and how it was tested.

How the forecast is calculated, why each part counts as much as it does, and how the forecast will sharpen as the election gets closer.

The presidential model

The forecast is built in three steps. First it estimates the national popular vote: each party's share of the votes cast for the two of them, written here as a margin, so D+4.0 means the Democrat ahead by four points. Next it places each state against that national figure. Last, it simulates the election times, allowing for the ways the estimates could be wrong, and counts how often each side reaches 270 electoral votes.

The weights in step 1 apply to the estimate of the national vote, not to the chance of winning. A model with half the weight moves the national margin by half as much as it would alone; how much that changes the chance of winning depends on everything else in the simulation.

    Where the structural models come from

    The Margin reads Tipping Point, a sister site that runs two published academic models, and Tipping Point does not read The Margin. The two sites answer different questions: a share of the vote is not a chance of winning.

    Sources and limits

    The Margin is a personal, non-commercial project. It checks its sources many times a day and reruns the forecast when a poll or one of its sources has changed. Polls of past elections come from FiveThirtyEight's archive and the model data The Economist published; polling averages for 1972 to 2016 come from FiveThirtyEight's public data; expert ratings are Cook Political Report's and Sabato's Crystal Ball's; results are official counts. The national head-to-head polls, state starting points and presidential approval come from the Tipping Point Nowcast, which reads its polls from VoteHub and from PollResults.org. Polls of single states are read from PollResults.org, which serves The New York Times's poll data. Both sets of poll data are used under the CC BY 4.0 license; The Margin is not affiliated with or endorsed by VoteHub, PollResults.org or The New York Times. The code and data are in the repository.

    Limits worth knowing: pollsters are not rated, so each counts the same apart from sample size and recency. Prediction markets are shown for comparison and are not in the forecast or the back-tests. The back-tests include Tipping Point's state-level models, but with one forecast for each year standing in for every day of it, and for 2020 the approval model's entry is the forecast its authors published then, made with an earlier version of the model.
